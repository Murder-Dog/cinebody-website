<p>Crowdsourced video tends to fail in the same five ways. The ask is vague, so the clips are random. Nobody knows what they get for filming, so few people do. Rights come up after the best clip arrives. Half the footage is dark and shaky. And a pile of uploads sits unreviewed until the moment has passed.</p>
<p>Each of those is a decision you can make before launch. Here are the five to settle first. If you are still choosing where to run the campaign, start with <a href="/cinebody-blog/video-crowdsourcing-platforms">video crowdsourcing platforms</a>.</p>

<h2>1. A clear ask</h2>
<p>People film what you ask for. If you ask for "authentic moments," you get whatever they happen to point the phone at.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pick who is filming.</strong> Customers, employees, fans, event attendees. Each group has different time, access, and comfort on camera, and your ask should fit them.</li>
<li><strong>Say what you are making.</strong> "A 30-second vertical recap for paid social" tells people more than a campaign name.</li>
<li><strong>List a few specific shots.</strong> One ask per shot, most important first, so the clip you cannot cut without is the one you are most likely to get.</li>
<li><strong>Say how long it takes and when it is due.</strong> "Three clips, about ten minutes, by Friday."</li>
<li><strong>Write like a person.</strong> No marketing language. Fans do not want to decode a brief.</li>
</ul>
<p>For help writing the list, see <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-is-a-shot-list-and-how-do-i-write-one-for-a-ugc-campaign">What Is a Shot List?</a> and <a href="/cinebody-blog/start-your-shot-list-from-the-brief">Start Your Shot List From a Brief</a>.</p>

<h2>2. Incentives and payouts</h2>
<p>Some communities will film for the fun of it, or for the chance to be featured. Most need a reason, especially when the ask is real work. Decide the reward before you recruit anyone.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flat rate.</strong> A set amount for completing the shot list. Simple, and good when every participant matters.</li>
<li><strong>Per-clip bounty.</strong> Pay for each clip you accept. Good when you want volume and only use the best.</li>
<li><strong>Gift cards, product, or perks.</strong> Early access, merch, tickets. Often right for fans and customers.</li>
</ul>
<p>Whatever you pick, say it up front: what it pays, what counts as an accepted clip, and when people get paid. Vague rewards get vague effort.</p>
<p>One more thing to plan for. If you pay people or give them something of value, and their clips end up in ads where they talk about your product, the FTC expects that connection to be disclosed when viewers would not assume it. The same goes for contest entries posted publicly for a chance to win.</p>

<h2>3. Rights and releases up front</h2>
<p>The worst time to ask for usage rights is after a clip turns out to be the hero of the campaign. Collect permission when people sign up.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Name the uses.</strong> Channels (including paid ads), how long, where in the world, and whether you can edit the footage.</li>
<li><strong>Cover everyone on camera.</strong> A release from each recognizable person, and a parent or guardian's signature for anyone under 18. Or simply ask filmers to keep minors out of frame.</li>
<li><strong>Ask for no music while filming.</strong> A song playing in the background is not the filmer's to license, and it can sink an otherwise perfect clip.</li>
<li><strong>Mind the location.</strong> Filming on private property, including workplaces and venues, needs the owner's okay.</li>
</ul>
<p>The basics are in <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-are-usage-rights">What Are Usage Rights?</a>, and the full advertising checklist is in <a href="/cinebody-blog/photo-video-usage-rights">Photo and Video Usage Rights for Advertising</a>.</p>

<h2>4. Quality guidance</h2>
<p>Most people filming for a brand are not filmmakers, and they should not need to be. A few lines of guidance fix most problems before they happen.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Orientation.</strong> Tell them vertical or horizontal. Do not make them guess.</li>
<li><strong>Light.</strong> Face the window or the sun. Do not stand in front of it.</li>
<li><strong>Steady.</strong> Two hands, elbows in, and hold each shot for a few seconds.</li>
<li><strong>Sound.</strong> A quiet spot for anything spoken, and get close to the person talking.</li>
<li><strong>Lens.</strong> Wipe it first.</li>
</ul>
<p>An example clip beats a paragraph of instructions. For more, see <a href="/cinebody-blog/how-to-frame-video-on-smartphones">How to Frame Video on Smartphones</a> and <a href="/cinebody-blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-capturing-b-roll">Film B-Roll Editors Can Actually Use</a>.</p>

<h2>5. A review plan</h2>
<p>Footage reviewed a week later is footage nobody can reshoot. Decide before launch who reviews, how fast, and against what.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Review as clips arrive,</strong> while people are still on location and can try again.</li>
<li><strong>Use a short checklist.</strong> Clear sound, good light, in focus, framed right, on brief, releases in place, nothing in frame you cannot use.</li>
<li><strong>Talk back.</strong> Thank people, tell them what is working, and ask for one specific retake instead of a general "can you redo it?" Encouragement keeps people filming.</li>
<li><strong>Tie approval to payout,</strong> so the people doing good work get paid quickly.</li>
<li><strong>Know who cuts it and by when,</strong> so the footage becomes a video while the campaign still matters.</li>
</ul>
