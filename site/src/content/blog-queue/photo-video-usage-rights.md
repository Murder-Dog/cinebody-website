<p>Ads get pulled for reasons that have nothing to do with the creative. A song nobody licensed. A release that only covered organic social. A license that ran out three weeks ago while the ad kept spending. Every one of those is avoidable with a checklist.</p>
<p>This is that checklist, for anyone running photo or video in paid media. If you want the basics first (what usage rights are, the key terms, and who owns footage by default), start with <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-are-usage-rights">What Are Usage Rights?</a></p>
<p><em>General guidance, not legal advice. Rules vary by state and country, and a lawyer should review the contracts for any significant campaign.</em></p>

<h2>Start with the plan: deliverables, duration, distribution</h2>
<p>Every clearance below depends on three answers. Write them down before you book anyone.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Deliverables.</strong> What you are making: a 30-second spot, six cutdowns, stills, alternate versions. Talent and music are often priced by how many ads they appear in, so list the cutdowns from the start.</li>
<li><strong>Duration.</strong> How long the ads will run. If you might extend, negotiate the renewal option and its price now, while you have leverage.</li>
<li><strong>Distribution.</strong> Where it runs: organic social, paid social, web, connected TV, broadcast, out-of-home. A national TV buy and a regional social campaign are priced very differently.</li>
</ul>

<h2>People on camera: releases and minors</h2>
<figure><img src="/blog/photo-video-usage-rights/img-1.webp" alt="Bronze statue of Lady Justice holding scales and a sword against a gray wall"></figure>
<ul>
<li><strong>Get a signed release from every recognizable person.</strong> Most states let people control commercial use of their name and likeness. New York's statute, for example, covers a person's name, portrait, picture, or voice used for advertising without written consent.</li>
<li><strong>Match the release to the plan.</strong> Media, term, territory, edits, and paid use. A release that says "social media" may not stretch to connected TV.</li>
<li><strong>Voice counts.</strong> Voice-over artists are licensing their voice the same way on-camera talent license their likeness.</li>
<li><strong>Professional talent.</strong> Actors, models, and voice talent, especially union performers, usually work under contracts that set fees by use and time. Give their agents the full plan when you book.</li>
<li><strong>Minors cannot consent for themselves.</strong> Get a parent or guardian's signature for anyone under 18. The FTC also treats advertising aimed at children as an area of special concern.</li>
<li><strong>Crowds and events.</strong> The more identifiable and prominent a person is, the more you need their release. Post clear filming notices and get releases from anyone you feature.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Locations, art, and buildings</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Private property.</strong> Get a signed location agreement that covers filming and using the footage, not just access for the day. Public locations may need a film permit, so check local rules.</li>
<li><strong>Buildings.</strong> In the US, the copyright in a building's design does not stop you from photographing or filming it if it is in or ordinarily visible from a public place.</li>
<li><strong>Artwork.</strong> Murals, sculptures, posters, and art on the walls are copyrighted works. If a piece is prominent in an ad, get the artist's permission or keep it out of the frame.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Music</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Most songs need two licenses.</strong> A sync license covers the composition (usually from the publisher). A master use license covers the specific recording (usually from the label). A cover version still needs the composition cleared.</li>
<li><strong>Popular tracks are priced by use.</strong> Media, term, territory, and how prominent the music is in the spot.</li>
<li><strong>Read stock music licenses too.</strong> Check that the license covers every channel you plan to use, including paid ads and broadcast.</li>
<li><strong>Music in customer and creator clips is not cleared.</strong> A song playing in someone's video is not theirs to license. Replace the audio or license the track. Do not assume a song available inside a social app is cleared for your ad.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Logos and trademarks in frame</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Incidental is usually fine.</strong> Trademark claims generally turn on consumer confusion or a false suggestion of endorsement, and a logo passing through the background rarely creates either.</li>
<li><strong>Featured is different.</strong> When another brand's product becomes a focal point, or the framing suggests a partnership, remove it, swap it, or get permission. Keep competitors out entirely.</li>
<li><strong>Check where it airs.</strong> Broadcasters and platforms can have clearance standards stricter than the law.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Creators, influencers, and paid usage</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>A creator's post is not your ad.</strong> The contract has to say whether you can repost it, run it as a paid ad, or cut it into other ads, and for how long.</li>
<li><strong>Whitelisting, now usually called partnership ads.</strong> This means running ads from the creator's handle. On Meta, partnership ads need permission from the partner whose handle the ad runs from, and they can revoke it at any time. On TikTok, Spark Ads use an authorization code the creator generates for a specific post, with a set authorization period.</li>
<li><strong>Price paid usage separately.</strong> The fee for making a post and the fee for putting spend behind it are different line items. Add exclusivity if you need it.</li>
<li><strong>Require disclosure.</strong> The FTC expects a paid or gifted relationship to be disclosed clearly, in the video itself, not only in a caption. A platform's built-in label may not be enough on its own. Brands are expected to instruct creators and check what gets posted.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Renewals and expirations</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Log every asset.</strong> The clip, the people in it, the music, the license, media, territory, start date, end date, and renewal price.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the signed documents with the asset,</strong> not in one person's inbox.</li>
<li><strong>Set reminders well before a term ends,</strong> so renewing is a decision rather than a surprise.</li>
<li><strong>When a term ends, pull it everywhere.</strong> Paused ad sets, the website, sales decks, email templates, in-store screens.</li>
</ul>
