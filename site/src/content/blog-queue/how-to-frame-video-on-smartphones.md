<p>Most phone video that looks amateur does not have a gear problem. It has a framing problem: the subject is jammed against the top edge, the horizon tilts, a lamp grows out of someone's head, and every shot is the same distance away.</p>
<p>Framing costs nothing and takes seconds. Here is what to check before you press record.</p>
<h2>Pick the orientation for where the video will run</h2>
<p>Decide this first, because you cannot fix it later without losing most of the picture. A vertical clip cropped to horizontal keeps a thin slice of the middle. The reverse is just as bad.</p>
<p>Short-form feeds are built for vertical. TikTok recommends 9:16 vertical video for its in-feed ads, and Google calls 9:16 the best fit for YouTube Shorts. Instagram Reels accept anything from 1.91:1 to 9:16. A website player, a presentation, or a standard YouTube video is horizontal: YouTube's standard aspect ratio on a computer is 16:9.</p>
<p>If one shoot has to feed both, decide the orientation shot by shot and write it down. Guessing is how a great interview ends up in the wrong shape.</p>
<h2>Turn on the grid and use the thirds</h2>
<figure><img src="/blog/how-to-frame-video-on-smartphones/img-1.webp" alt="A hand holding a phone with the camera grid turned on while framing a rocky landscape at sunset"></figure>
<p>The grid splits the screen into nine boxes, two lines across and two lines down. On iPhone, open Settings, tap Camera, and turn on Grid and Level. On a Pixel, open the Camera app, tap Settings, then More Settings, then Grid type. Other Android phones keep a grid option in the camera settings, usually under a similar name.</p>
<p>Use the lines instead of the center. Put a person's eyes near the top line. Put a product on one of the vertical lines and let the open side breathe. Put the horizon on the top or bottom line, not through the middle, and use the level so it stays flat.</p>
<p>Center framing still has its place. A person talking straight to camera in a vertical video, or a symmetrical doorway, can sit dead center. Just make it a choice.</p>
<h2>Leave headroom and lead room</h2>
<p><strong>Headroom</strong> is the space above someone's head. Too much and they look small and sunk to the bottom. Too little and their forehead touches the edge. A little space, with the eyes on the top line, is the safe default. In a tight close-up, cropping the top of the head is fine. Cropping the chin is not.</p>
<p><strong>Lead room</strong> is space in the direction someone looks or moves. If a person faces left, frame them on the right so the open space is in front of them. If someone walks across the frame, leave room ahead of them, not behind.</p>
<h2>Hold the phone at eye level</h2>
<p>Put the lens at the subject's eye height. For someone sitting, that is lower than you think. A phone held below the chin looks up their nose. A phone held high looks down on them and makes them seem small.</p>
<p>Eye level reads as a normal conversation, which is what most brand video wants. Break the rule on purpose: go low to make an athlete or a building feel big, or go high to show a table of product or a crowd.</p>
<p>Once the frame is right, lock it. On iPhone, touch and hold the subject until AE/AF Lock appears, so focus and exposure stop shifting as people move. Tap the screen again to unlock.</p>
<h2>Clean up the background</h2>
<p>Look past the subject before you roll. The background is half the frame, and viewers read all of it.</p>
<ul>
<li>Check for things growing out of heads: poles, plants, lamps, door frames.</li>
<li>Move clutter, trash cans, and anything with a competing logo.</li>
<li>Pull the subject a few steps away from the wall. It adds depth and keeps shadows off the wall behind them.</li>
<li>Turn so a bright window is beside the subject, not behind them, or you get a silhouette.</li>
</ul>
<p>Often the fix is not moving the stuff. It is moving the phone a step left or right until the background clears.</p>
<h2>Mix wide, medium, and close</h2>
<p>A video built from one shot size feels flat, even when every shot is framed well. Editors need variety to cut.</p>
<p><strong>Wide</strong> shows where you are: the store, the trail, the room. <strong>Medium</strong>, roughly waist up, is the workhorse for people talking and doing. <strong>Close</strong> is hands, faces, product details, and reactions.</p>
<p>For any moment that matters, grab at least two sizes. Walk closer when you can. It changes the feel of a shot more than zooming does.</p>

For people talking on camera, the <a href="/cinebody-blog/5-steps-to-recording-interviews-iphone">ABCDE interview checklist</a> covers sound, light, and setup.</p>
<h2>Check the frame before every take</h2>
<p>Orientation. Grid on. Eyes on the top line, with room to look into. Lens at eye level. Nothing growing out of anyone's head. A wide, a medium, and a close.</p>
