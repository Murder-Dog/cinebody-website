<p>If your video only makes sense with the sound on, a lot of people will never get the point. Captions fix that, but only if they are accurate, readable, and placed where the app's buttons will not cover them.</p>
<p>Here is how to get captions right on short brand video, from the choice of caption type to the first two seconds of the cut.</p>
<h2>Why captions matter</h2>
<p>Start with accessibility. The World Health Organization estimates that over 430 million people, more than 5% of the world's population, need rehabilitation for disabling hearing loss. Without captions, your video leaves them out.</p>
<p>The web's main accessibility standard says the same thing. The W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) require captions for prerecorded audio in video at Level A, the baseline level. WCAG also notes that good captions do more than transcribe dialogue: they identify who is speaking and include meaningful sounds, like laughter or a crowd cheering, when those sounds matter.</p>
<p>Then there is everyone else: people on a train, in an open office, or next to a sleeping kid. Captions let them follow along without reaching for headphones. Write captions for the viewer who cannot hear, and they will work for the viewer who chose not to.</p>
<h2>Burned-in captions vs platform captions</h2>
<p>There are two kinds, and each has a job.</p>
<p><strong>Burned-in captions</strong> (also called open captions) are part of the video image. They show up everywhere the file goes, look exactly how you designed them, and cannot be turned off. The tradeoffs: viewers cannot hide them or resize them, you have to re-export to fix a typo, and the app's own interface can land on top of them.</p>
<p><strong>Platform captions</strong> (closed captions) are a text layer the app displays over the video. Most major platforms can generate them from speech:</p>
<ul>
<li>YouTube creates automatic captions with speech recognition, and says creators should always review them and edit anything that was not transcribed properly. You can edit them in YouTube Studio.</li>
<li>Instagram can turn on automatic closed captions for reels, which write out the speech as text at the bottom of the reel.</li>
<li>TikTok lets creators add auto captions on the editing page and edit the text once it is generated. Viewers can turn them off.</li>
</ul>
<p>A practical rule: burn captions in when the look is part of the creative or the file will be posted in many places, and use platform captions when viewer control matters, for example on longer videos on YouTube or your website. Do not stack both on the same video. Two sets of words on screen is harder to read than one.</p>
<h2>Make them readable on a phone</h2>
<h3>Size</h3>
<p>Check captions on an actual phone at arm's length, not on a big monitor. If you squint, they are too small. Use a clean, heavy sans-serif and avoid thin or script fonts.</p>
<h3>Contrast</h3>
<p>Captions have to read over bright sky, white walls, and busy crowds. Use light text with a dark outline, a drop shadow, or a semi-transparent box behind it. For a benchmark, WCAG's minimum contrast for regular text on a screen is 4.5:1 against the background. Test your captions over the brightest and busiest shot in the video, not the easiest one.</p>
<h3>Line length</h3>
<p>Short lines are easier to read in a quick scroll. Netflix's English subtitle guide caps lines at 42 characters and subtitles at two lines. On vertical video, where the frame is narrow, aim shorter than that. Break lines at natural pauses, not in the middle of a phrase, and keep each caption on screen long enough to read comfortably.</p>
<h3>Placement clear of on-screen buttons</h3>
<p>Vertical feeds cover parts of the frame with usernames, descriptions, and like, comment, and share buttons. Meta's guidance for Reels ads is to keep roughly the top 14%, the bottom 35%, and 6% on each side free of text and key elements. That puts burned-in captions higher in the frame than the bottom edge, where they often end up by default. Keep them off faces and any on-screen text too.</p>
<h2>Check accuracy before you post</h2>
<p>Automatic captions are a draft. YouTube's own help page warns that they can misrepresent speech because of mispronunciations, accents, dialects, or background noise. A few habits catch most errors:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Watch the whole video once with the sound off</strong>, reading only the captions. If the story does not hold up, fix the words.</li>
<li><strong>Check names, brand terms, and product names first.</strong> Speech recognition often gets these wrong, and they are the most embarrassing mistakes.</li>
<li><strong>Check numbers and prices</strong> against the script or the source.</li>
<li><strong>Check timing.</strong> Words should appear as they are spoken, not a beat late.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the speaker's meaning.</strong> Trimming filler is fine. Changing what someone said is not.</li>
</ul>
<p>Clean audio makes all of this easier. If you are recording people talking, the <a href="/cinebody-blog/5-steps-to-recording-interviews-iphone">ABCDE interview checklist</a> covers getting clear sound on a phone.</p>
<h2>Open with something that works in silence</h2>
<p>Captions help, but the first seconds still have to earn attention without sound. Open on something visual: a face mid-reaction, a product in use, movement, or a clear on-screen line that says what the video is about. Avoid a slow logo or a wide shot of a room while someone says hello.</p>
<p>A quick test: mute the video and watch the first three seconds. If you would not know why to keep watching, recut the open. For more on getting shots that carry a story without words, see <a href="/cinebody-blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-capturing-b-roll">Film B-Roll Editors Can Actually Use</a>.</p>
<h2>Captions are part of the cut</h2>
<p>Treat captions as part of the edit, not a last-minute export setting. On Cinebody, the finished cut is built with music and captions, and you review, tweak, and approve it before it goes out, so a sound-off check fits into the same review.</p>
