<p>You have a great clip. A customer filmed it, or an employee did, or a creator posted it and tagged you. Before it goes into an ad, you need to know one thing: are you allowed to use it that way?</p>
<p>Usage rights answer that question. They are the permission to use a photo, a video, a song, or a person's likeness, spelled out by where, for how long, and how. This is the plain version. For the full advertising checklist (releases, minors, music, logos, paid social), read <a href="/cinebody-blog/photo-video-usage-rights">Photo and Video Usage Rights for Advertising</a>.</p>
<p><em>This is general guidance, not legal advice. For a specific campaign or contract, talk to a lawyer.</em></p>

<h2>Owning a clip and using a clip are different things</h2>
<p>Copyright covers the footage itself. Under US law, it belongs first to the person who created it. Usage rights are what that owner lets someone else do with it. A brand can use footage it does not own, as long as the owner has given permission that covers the use.</p>
<p>There is a second layer: the people in the shot. Most US states give people control over the commercial use of their name and likeness. So even when you own a video outright, you still want permission from the recognizable people in it before it runs as an ad.</p>

<h2>The key terms in any usage agreement</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Channels and media.</strong> Where it can run: organic social, paid social, your website, email, connected TV, broadcast, billboards, in-store screens. Name each one, or agree on "all media" knowingly.</li>
<li><strong>Term.</strong> How long you can use it. A set period, such as 12 months from first run, or perpetual. Professional talent often push back on perpetual, or charge a lot more for it.</li>
<li><strong>Territory.</strong> Where in the world. One country, a region, or worldwide.</li>
<li><strong>Exclusivity.</strong> Whether the owner can license the same content to others, or whether the person on camera can appear for a competitor, during your term.</li>
<li><strong>Organic vs. paid.</strong> Posting a clip to your feed and putting ad spend behind it are different uses. Many agreements price paid use separately, so always say whether it is included.</li>
<li><strong>Edits.</strong> Whether you can trim, crop, caption, add music, or cut the footage together with other material.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Who owns footage a customer or employee films</h2>
<p><strong>Employees.</strong> Work an employee creates within the scope of their job is a "work made for hire," and the employer is treated as the author. A marketing coordinator filming a product demo on the clock is the clear case. An employee filming their weekend trail run on a personal phone is less clear, so get permission in writing anyway.</p>
<p><strong>Customers, fans, and freelancers.</strong> They own what they film. Asking for a video, or even paying for it, does not make you the owner by itself. Under US law, a transfer of copyright ownership has to be in a signed writing. A simpler non-exclusive permission to use a clip does not transfer ownership at all, but you still want the terms written down so both sides agree on what was granted.</p>
<p><strong>Creators and influencers.</strong> The same as customers, plus whatever their contract says. A creator posting about your product does not give you the right to run that post as your ad.</p>

<h2>How to get permission</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Ask before you use it.</strong> Not after the ad is live.</li>
<li><strong>Be specific.</strong> Name the clip, the channels, the term, the territory, and whether it will run as a paid ad.</li>
<li><strong>Get it in writing.</strong> A signed release or license, or an e-signed form. A thumbs-up in a DM is a thin record if anyone remembers it differently later.</li>
<li><strong>Cover the people on camera.</strong> A release from each recognizable person, and a parent or guardian's signature for anyone under 18.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a record.</strong> Who agreed, to what, and when it ends. Set a reminder before it does.</li>
</ol>

<h2>A quick example</h2>
<p>A customer posts an unboxing video and tags your brand. You want to use it in a paid social ad in the US for six months.</p>
<ul>
<li>Ask the customer for permission to use that specific video in paid social ads, in the US, for six months, with light edits.</li>
<li>Confirm they filmed it, and that anyone else recognizable in the frame agrees too.</li>
<li>Check the audio. A popular song playing in the background is not the customer's to license.</li>
<li>Get the yes in writing, log the end date, and decide before it lapses whether to renew or pull the ad.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Plan rights into the shoot</h2>
<p>Rights are easiest to settle before anyone presses record. If your employees, customers, and fans are filming for you, tell them up front how the footage will be used and collect permission when they join the project, not weeks later when one clip turns out to be the one you want to put money behind.</p>
