<p>Everyone has seen the stock clip of a team laughing around a laptop. Your audience has too. Generic stock fills a gap in the edit, but it says nothing about your brand, and anyone can license the same clip.</p>
<p>The better option is footage of your own people, places, and product, filmed once and reused for years. Here is how to build that library so it is actually usable.</p>
<h2>Why your own library beats generic stock</h2>
<p><strong>It is recognizable.</strong> Your storefront, your warehouse, your product in a real customer's hands. Viewers who know the brand see something true. Viewers who do not can still tell it is not a set.</p>
<p><strong>Nobody else has it.</strong> A licensed clip can turn up in a competitor's ad next week. Your own footage cannot.</p>
<p><strong>It matches.</strong> Clips filmed in your spaces, with your people, cut together with your interviews and campaigns without a jarring jump in look.</p>
<p><strong>It builds up.</strong> Every shoot adds to it. A library started now makes next year's projects faster, because the cutaways already exist.</p>
<p><strong>You set the terms.</strong> Stock licenses come with conditions on how and where a clip can be used. With your own footage, the limits are the ones you agree on with the people in it, which is why releases matter so much.</p>
<h2>What to collect</h2>
<figure><img src="/blog/ugc-stock-footage/img-1.webp" alt="A woman holds an open box of skincare products toward the camera"></figure>
<p>Aim for evergreen clips that can cover a line of voiceover or bridge two scenes in almost any edit.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Places.</strong> Exteriors, entrances, signage, the main room, the view from the front desk. A wide first, then a few details.</li>
<li><strong>Product.</strong> Hands opening, using, and setting down the product. Close-ups with clean backgrounds. The product in real homes, trucks, gyms, or offices.</li>
<li><strong>People at work.</strong> Employees doing the job: building, packing, serving, talking with a customer. Faces and hands.</li>
<li><strong>Customers and fans.</strong> Real people using the product where they actually use it, and genuine reactions.</li>
<li><strong>Culture.</strong> Team meetings, celebrations, volunteer days, events. These date quickly, so capture them often.</li>
<li><strong>Movement and texture.</strong> Walking shots, doors opening, slow pans across a shelf, a few seconds of the room with nobody talking.</li>
</ul>
<p>Film more than one take of each and hold every shot for at least five to ten seconds so an editor has room. If a clip might run as a vertical social post and in a horizontal video, capture both orientations. For technique, see <a href="/cinebody-blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-capturing-b-roll">Film B-Roll Editors Can Actually Use</a>.</p>
<p>Events, launches, and ordinary workdays are all good collection days. You can also schedule a shoot whose only purpose is to fill the library.</p>
<h2>Get permissions before you need them</h2>
<p>A library is only as useful as the rights attached to it. Sort this out when the footage is filmed, not when a campaign is due.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Get a release from everyone identifiable on camera.</strong> Spell out where the footage may run (social, website, paid ads, TV), for how long, and in which regions.</li>
<li><strong>Be clear about ownership.</strong> Footage an employee films as part of the job is usually treated differently from footage a customer films on their own phone. Put who owns what, and what the brand may do with it, in writing.</li>
<li><strong>Take extra care with minors.</strong> A parent or guardian should sign.</li>
<li><strong>Watch the background.</strong> Recognizable third-party logos, artwork, private property, and music playing in the room can all limit where a clip can run.</li>
<li><strong>Plan for people moving on.</strong> Decide in the release what happens to footage of an employee after they leave.</li>
</ul>
<p>Our posts <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-are-usage-rights">What Are Usage Rights?</a> and <a href="/cinebody-blog/photo-video-usage-rights">Key Usage Rights for Video and Photo</a> go deeper. This is general guidance, not legal advice. Have your own counsel review your release forms.</p>
<h2>Tag and organize it so people can find it</h2>
<p>A folder called "B-roll final 2" is where footage goes to be forgotten. Decide on a structure once and use it everywhere.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A consistent file name.</strong> Date, location, subject, and a short description, in the same order every time.</li>
<li><strong>Core tags.</strong> Who is in it, where it was filmed, what is happening, which product is shown, orientation, and season.</li>
<li><strong>Rights tags.</strong> Release on file, approved uses, and an expiration date if the release has one. Keep the signed release linked to the clip.</li>
<li><strong>A status.</strong> Approved, needs review, or do not use. Clips with an old logo, a discontinued product, or an expired release get flagged, not deleted.</li>
<li><strong>One owner.</strong> Someone who adds new footage, checks the tags, and prunes the library every few months.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Reuse it everywhere</h2>
<p>Once the library is organized, it pays off in more places than you expect.</p>
<ul>
<li>Cutaways under interviews and testimonials.</li>
<li>Short social cuts built entirely from library clips.</li>
<li>Paid ad variations, when you need many versions fast.</li>
<li>Website headers, sales decks, recruiting pages, and internal presentations.</li>
<li>Patching a new edit when one shot from a shoot did not come back.</li>
</ul>
<p>Keep it growing without a separate project. Add two or three library shots to the end of every shot list you send, after the shots the current edit needs.</p>
