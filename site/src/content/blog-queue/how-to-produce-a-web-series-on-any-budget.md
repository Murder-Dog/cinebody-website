<p>A web series does not need a crew, a script, or a studio budget. It needs a format you can repeat, real people with stories worth following, and a plan that gets footage in every week without heroics.</p>
<p>The budget-friendly version is documentary style, filmed on phones by the people in it. Here is how to build one.</p>
<h2>Pick a format you can repeat</h2>
<p>A scripted series needs writers, actors, rehearsals, and the same people and locations for every episode. That gets expensive to sustain. A documentary series follows real people doing real things, so the planning is lighter and the footage feels honest.</p>
<p>Choose a format you can explain in one sentence and repeat for ten episodes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Day in the life.</strong> One person, one day: their work, sport, or routine.</li>
<li><strong>Behind the scenes.</strong> How a product, event, or team actually gets made.</li>
<li><strong>The journey.</strong> A few people working toward the same thing, like a race, an opening, or a season.</li>
<li><strong>One question.</strong> The same question, answered by different people in different places.</li>
</ul>
<p>Then set an episode length and a release cadence you can hold. A tight weekly episode beats an ambitious monthly one that slips. Decide early where the series will run, since that sets the orientation for every shot.</p>
<h2>Cast real people, not performers</h2>
<p>The stars of a documentary series are people who already care: employees, customers, athletes, members, fans. They know the subject, and they do not need to act.</p>
<p>Start with people you already know. Ask your team who has a story. Post a call on your own channels and ask interested people to send a short clip about why they would be a good fit. That clip tells you two things at once: whether they are comfortable on camera, and whether they can film themselves.</p>
<p>Two or three main characters is enough to start. Offer something fair for their time, like product, a stipend, or a trip. Many people who say yes mostly want to be part of the story.</p>
<p>Brands have done this with Cinebody before. The Outdoor Group made Homelife, a behind-the-scenes look at some of hunting television's most recognizable names, then produced a second series, Elite Dealer, at the same time. On Yoga Journal's Live Be Yoga Tour, the yogis became the film crew.</p>
<h2>Structure episodes before anyone films</h2>
<p>Write a series outline, even if the show has no planned end. Map the first five episodes. Give each one a single focus, one piece of the bigger story, so filmers know what they are capturing and editors know what they are building.</p>
<p>A simple shape works for most formats:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Open</strong> on a moment that sets up the goal or question.</li>
<li><strong>Middle</strong> with the work, the attempt, or the conversation.</li>
<li><strong>Close</strong> on a result, a reflection, or a hint of next week.</li>
</ol>
<p>Inside that shape, give people room. As long as what they film ladders up to the episode's focus, the unplanned moments are often the best part.</p>
<h2>Plan capture for each episode</h2>
<p>Turn every episode into a short shot list for each person. Keep it to a handful of asks, one per shot, most important first. <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-is-a-shot-list-and-how-do-i-write-one-for-a-ugc-campaign">What Is a Shot List and How Do I Write One for a UGC Campaign?</a> covers the rules.</p>
<p>Most documentary episodes need three kinds of footage:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The story:</strong> a line to camera or a short interview about what is happening. The <a href="/cinebody-blog/5-steps-to-recording-interviews-iphone">ABCDE interview checklist</a> helps here.</li>
<li><strong>The action:</strong> the thing they are doing, start to finish.</li>
<li><strong>The cutaways:</strong> an establishing shot, details, and reactions. See <a href="/cinebody-blog/five-tips-for-great-b-roll-filming">Five B-Roll Shots to Grab Every Time</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>Give every list a deadline tied to the edit. Open-ended asks turn into missed episodes.</p>
<h2>Shoot remotely without losing the thread</h2>
<p>When your cast is spread out, you direct from a distance. Three habits keep it on track.</p>
<p><strong>Brief once, clearly.</strong> Send the episode focus and the shot list together, in plain words, with the orientation on every shot.</p>
<p><strong>Watch footage as it arrives.</strong> Review the first clips the same day. If the audio is noisy or the wide is missing, ask while the person is still there. After they go home, it is a reshoot.</p>
<p><strong>Keep the originals.</strong> Collect full-quality files through an upload link or shared drive, not email attachments.</p>

<h2>Keep an edit cadence</h2>
<p>A series lives on consistency. Set an edit rhythm that matches your release schedule, and protect it.</p>
<ul>
<li>Start editing when the first clips land, not after the last one.</li>
<li>Build a template: same open, same title style, same music bed, similar length. Each episode gets faster.</li>
<li>Stay an episode or two ahead of release so one late filmer does not break the schedule.</li>
<li>Pull short cutdowns for social while you are already in the footage.</li>
</ul>
