<p>Most event recaps fail for the same reason: nobody decided what to film until the doors opened. The fix is a little planning before, a clear list of moments during, and a fast, channel-specific edit after.</p>
<p>This playbook works for a conference, a store opening, a team offsite, or a customer night. Scale it up or down, but keep the order.</p>
<h2>Before the event: plan the shots, the people, and the permissions</h2>
<h3>Write a short shot list</h3>
<p>Ten to fifteen shots is plenty. Start from what the recap needs to say, then list the moments that prove it. A typical list:</p>
<ul>
<li>The venue from outside, before anyone arrives</li>
<li>Signage, branding, and the room being set up</li>
<li>People arriving and checking in</li>
<li>The key moment: the keynote line, the ribbon, the launch, the award</li>
<li>The crowd reacting to that moment</li>
<li>Two or three quick interviews with attendees</li>
<li>Details: name badges, food, product on the table, hands on a demo</li>
<li>The room at its fullest, and the last people leaving</li>
</ul>
<p>Put must-haves at the top, in plain words. If you have not written one before, <a href="/cinebody-blog/start-your-shot-list-from-the-brief">Start Your Shot List From a Brief, Not a Blank Page</a> walks through it.</p>
<h3>Decide who films</h3>
<p>Name the filmers and give each one a lane. One person on the stage and the key moment. One person roaming the room for arrivals, details, and reactions. One person on interviews. On a small event, one or two people can cover all three if the list is clear.</p>
<p>Employees and attendees who already care about the event often film more natural moments than an outside crew, because people relax around them. Send them the list the day before, not at the door.</p>
<h3>Handle releases and filming notices</h3>
<p>Get signed releases from anyone you plan to interview or feature up close. For the wider crowd, post clear notices at the entrance and on the registration page saying the event is being filmed and how the footage will be used. Give people an easy way to opt out, like a colored lanyard or a quiet word with a staff member, and tell your filmers what that signal means.</p>
<p>Check the venue's rules on filming too. Some spaces restrict it or require permission. For the details on releases, minors, music, and paid use, see <a href="/cinebody-blog/photo-video-usage-rights">Photo and Video Usage Rights for Advertising</a>. This is general guidance, not legal advice.</p>
<h2>During the event: catch the moments in order</h2>
<h3>Arrivals</h3>
<p>Film the empty venue first, then the doors opening and the first people walking in. Arrivals give the recap its opening: energy building, people greeting each other, lanyards going on. Hold each shot for five seconds or more so the editor has room to cut.</p>
<h3>Signage and setting</h3>
<p>Get the name of the event on screen early. Film the main sign, the stage backdrop, and a few branded details. These double as clean cutaways later. Grab them before the room fills and people block the view.</p>
<h3>The key moment</h3>
<p>Every event has one: the announcement, the reveal, the speech, the countdown. Know when it happens and have a filmer in position a few minutes early with a clear line of sight and a charged phone. Start recording before it begins and keep rolling after it ends. This is the shot you cannot get again.</p>
<h3>Reactions</h3>
<p>While one person films the stage, another turns to the crowd. Faces, applause, people leaning in, someone filming on their own phone. Reactions are what make a recap feel like you were there. Keep rolling a beat after the peak, because the best reactions often come a second late.</p>
<p>For how to shoot all of this so an editor can use it, read <a href="/cinebody-blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-capturing-b-roll">Film B-Roll Editors Can Actually Use</a> and the quick version, <a href="/cinebody-blog/five-tips-for-great-b-roll-filming">Five B-Roll Shots to Grab Every Time</a>.</p>
<h3>Quick interviews</h3>
<p>Short and easy wins here. Pull someone a few steps away from the loudest part of the room, put the light in front of them, and get the phone close enough to hear them clearly. Ask one or two open questions:</p>
<ul>
<li>"What brought you here today?"</li>
<li>"What will you remember from tonight?"</li>
<li>"Tell me about the moment when..."</li>
</ul>
<p>Thirty seconds of a real answer beats three minutes of rambling. Our <a href="/cinebody-blog/5-steps-to-recording-interviews-iphone">ABCDE interview checklist</a> covers audio, light, and the rest in one pass.</p>
<h2>After the event: cut fast, then cut for each channel</h2>
<h3>Turn it around while people still care</h3>
<p>A recap is most useful while attendees are still talking about the event. Have filmers upload before they leave the venue, not next week. Review footage as it comes in, pull the best clip of the key moment, and publish a first short cut as soon as you can, even if the fuller version comes later.</p>
<h3>Make one cut per channel</h3>
<p>One long recap posted everywhere rarely works. Plan a few versions from the same footage:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A vertical short for social feeds.</strong> Open on the key moment or the biggest reaction, not the venue exterior. YouTube treats square or vertical videos up to three minutes as Shorts.</li>
<li><strong>A longer recap for your website, YouTube, or internal channels.</strong> This is where arrivals, the setting, interviews, and the full arc of the day belong.</li>
<li><strong>A single interview clip or quote.</strong> One attendee saying one strong thing works well on LinkedIn or in a follow-up email.</li>
<li><strong>A teaser for next year.</strong> Save your best reactions. They sell the next event better than any description.</li>
</ul>
<p>Add captions to every version so the recap still lands for anyone watching with the sound off. <a href="/cinebody-blog/captions-for-sound-off-video">Captions That Work When the Sound Is Off</a> covers how.</p>
