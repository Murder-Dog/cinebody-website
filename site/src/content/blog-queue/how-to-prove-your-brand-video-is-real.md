<p>&quot;Is this real?&quot; used to be a question for newsrooms. Now it shows up in brand reviews, legal checks, and client emails. If a real customer is on camera saying your product changed their morning, you want to be able to show that a real customer said it.</p>
<p>The good news is that proof of real footage is becoming a normal, checkable thing. The catch is that the word &quot;proof&quot; covers a few different ideas, and they do not all prove the same thing. Here is how to tell them apart.</p>
<h2>Why this matters now</h2>
<p>The big platforms are labeling generated and altered video. Meta shows &quot;AI info&quot; labels when it detects industry-standard signals or when a creator discloses AI use. TikTok labels AI-generated content automatically when a file carries Content Credentials, even if it was made somewhere else. YouTube requires creators to disclose realistic altered or synthetic content. In Europe, the AI Act's transparency rules took effect on August 2, 2026, and require clear labels on deepfakes. We covered the details in <a href="/cinebody-blog/will-ai-video-hurt-your-reach">Will AI Video Hurt Your Reach?</a></p>
<p>Labels change how people watch. A testimonial with an AI tag is not much of a testimonial. So the useful question is no longer &quot;did we use AI?&quot; It is &quot;could we show where this footage came from if someone asked?&quot;</p>
<h2>Two kinds of proof</h2>
<p>Most of what you will hear about falls into one of two buckets.</p>
<p><strong>Capture signatures</strong> are made by the camera at the moment of capture. The device signs what it recorded, so later you can check that the file came from that camera and see what changed since.</p>
<p><strong>Chain of custody</strong> starts when a file arrives somewhere. It records when it arrived, where it came from, and whether it has changed since. It does not claim to know what the camera saw. It proves the file you are looking at is the same file that was handed over.</p>
<p>Both are valuable. They answer different questions, and the honest move is to never let one stand in for the other.</p>
<h2>Capture signatures: C2PA, Google, and Apple</h2>
<p>The main open standard here is C2PA, from the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity. Its Content Credentials work like a nutrition label for a file: a signed record of where it came from and how it was edited. Camera makers, software companies, and platforms support it.</p>
<p>Phones are starting to sign at capture. Google's Pixel 10 attaches Content Credentials to every JPEG photo taken in the Pixel Camera app. Apple introduced Reference Image on iPhone 18 Pro, an opt-in mode that signs what the sensor sees and keeps an unalterable reference next to the photo you share (<a href="/cinebody-blog/apple-reference-image-proof-of-real">more on Apple's Reference Image</a>).</p>
<p>YouTube shows a &quot;captured with a camera&quot; label when a video was recorded with tools that support Content Credentials and nothing in the path from capture to upload broke the chain.</p>
<p>Know the limits before you plan around them:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>It is mostly photos today.</strong> Pixel's and Apple's phone features cover photos, not video.</li>
<li><strong>It needs specific hardware or apps.</strong> The people filming for your brand carry whatever phone they already own.</li>
<li><strong>Edits can break the chain.</strong> A brand video is almost always edited, and some editing paths drop the credentials.</li>
<li><strong>It proves how a file was made, not whether the scene is true.</strong> A signed photo of a staged moment is still staged. Google says the same: Content Credentials show verifiable proof of how something was made, not a verdict on whether it is authentic.</li>
</ul>
<h2>What Cinebody's Proof of Capture does</h2>
<p>Cinebody takes the chain of custody approach, because it works for every clip no matter which phone filmed it.</p>
<p>Every clip gets a Proof of Capture the moment it lands in Cinebody: a signed receipt of when it arrived, the phone it came from, and a fingerprint of the untouched original. The receipt also keeps a short timeline: when the clip was uploaded, when the receipt was signed, and when the clip was used in a cut and delivered.</p>
<p>The receipt stays with the clip through the edit. When a cut is delivered, it carries a list of every source clip inside it, and each one links to its own receipt. The delivery shows how many of those clips check out.</p>
<p>Anyone can check a receipt. The public page needs no login and shows no filmer names. Someone technical can go further: fingerprint the original file themselves, compare it to the receipt, and check Cinebody's signature against a key Cinebody publishes. They do not have to take our word for it.</p>
<h2>What it does not prove</h2>
<p>Being precise here is the point.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>It does not prove what the footage shows.</strong> A receipt does not judge whether a scene was staged or whether what someone said is accurate.</li>
<li><strong>It does not prove a sensor captured it.</strong> It proves a file with exactly those bytes reached Cinebody at that moment, from that phone, and has not changed since.</li>
<li><strong>It is not a camera signature.</strong> Apple Reference Image and Google Pixel Content Credentials are separate technologies made by the phone. Proof of Capture does not depend on them.</li>
</ul>
<p>What it does give you is a record nobody can quietly swap. If a client asks whether the clip in the final cut is the clip a real person sent in, you can show them.</p>
<h2>A practical checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Keep the originals.</strong> Proof starts with the untouched file, not the export.</li>
<li><strong>Know who filmed it.</strong> Real people on camera means consent and usage rights. Our guide to <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-are-usage-rights">usage rights</a> covers the basics.</li>
<li><strong>Ask for receipts, not promises.</strong> Whoever supplies your footage should be able to show where each clip came from.</li>
<li><strong>Do not claim more than your proof shows.</strong> &quot;Every clip in this cut came from a real person's phone and is unchanged&quot; is strong. &quot;This proves everything in the video is true&quot; is not something any tool can say.</li>
<li><strong>Disclose when AI made anything that looks real.</strong> Real footage plus honest labels on the rest keeps you on the right side of platforms and audiences.</li>
</ol>
<p>Real people filming on real phones is still the strongest signal a brand has. Cinebody coaches the shoot and helps with the edit, but it never makes the footage. Proof of Capture just makes that easy to show.</p>
