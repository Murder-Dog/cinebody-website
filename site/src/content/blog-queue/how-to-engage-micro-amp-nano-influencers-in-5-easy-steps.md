<p>Smaller creators are often the better bet for a brand: tighter audiences, more trust, and a far more reasonable price. The hard part is not deciding to work with them. It is doing it well, one person at a time, without turning every partnership into a negotiation.</p>
<p>First, the labels. There is no official standard. Nano influencers are commonly defined as about 1,000 to 10,000 followers, and micro influencers as 10,000 to 100,000. Some agencies draw the lines lower, capping nano at 5,000 or micro at 50,000. Treat the numbers as a rough guide.</p>
<figure><img src="/blog/how-to-engage-micro-amp-nano-influencers-in-5-easy-steps/img-1.webp" alt="Chart of influencer tiers by follower count: nano 1k to 10k, micro 10k to 50k, mid-tier 50k to 500k, macro 500k to 1 million, mega 1 million plus"></figure>

<h2>Step 1: Choose for fit, not follower count</h2>
<p>A creator with 4,000 followers who all buy what you sell beats one with 80,000 who do not. Look at five things.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Audience.</strong> Who follows them and where those people live. Ask for a screenshot of their account analytics.</li>
<li><strong>Content.</strong> Watch ten posts, not one. Would you be proud to run their usual work next to your logo?</li>
<li><strong>Real engagement.</strong> Read the comments. Actual conversation is a good sign. Walls of generic praise are not.</li>
<li><strong>Existing love.</strong> Someone already posting about your product, unpaid, is the strongest signal there is.</li>
<li><strong>Brand safety.</strong> Scroll back a few months before you reach out.</li>
</ul>
<p>For places to look, see <a href="/cinebody-blog/the-top-5-platforms-to-find-nano-influencers">Where to Find Nano Influencers</a>.</p>

<h2>Step 2: Reach out like a person</h2>
<figure><img src="/blog/how-to-engage-micro-amp-nano-influencers-in-5-easy-steps/img-2.webp" alt="Three Instagram Stories inviting followers to message the brand and become content ambassadors"></figure>
<p>Many nano creators have day jobs. A clear, short message gets answered. A vague one gets ignored.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Make it personal.</strong> Name a specific post you liked and why it fits.</li>
<li><strong>Put the offer up front.</strong> What you want them to make, the timeline, what it pays, and how you plan to use it.</li>
<li><strong>Use the channel they list.</strong> The email in their bio, or a DM if that is all there is.</li>
<li><strong>Follow up once.</strong> Then let it go.</li>
</ul>
<p>You can also invite people in. A post or Story on your own channels asking fans to apply often surfaces creators you would never find in a search.</p>

<h2>Step 3: Write a brief that leaves creative room</h2>
<p>People follow a creator for their voice. A script erases it. A good brief separates what is fixed from what is theirs.</p>
<p><strong>Fixed:</strong> the one message, product details that must be accurate, claims they cannot make, disclosure requirements, format (vertical, length), and deadlines.</p>
<p><strong>Theirs:</strong> the hook, the setting, the tone, and how they say it.</p>
<p>Share a few examples of what good looks like instead of lines to read. Agree on one round of feedback before they start, so nobody is surprised. If you are writing the brief from scratch, <a href="/cinebody-blog/start-your-shot-list-from-the-brief">Start Your Shot List From a Brief</a> walks through turning intent into filmable asks.</p>

<h2>Step 4: Pay fairly and settle the rights</h2>
<p><strong>Compensation.</strong> Cash, free product, an affiliate commission, or a mix. Free product alone can work for some nano creators, but it still counts as compensation. The FTC says a free or discounted product is a material connection that has to be disclosed.</p>
<p><strong>Disclosure.</strong> Ask for plain words like "ad" or "sponsored," placed where people will see them. In a video, the disclosure belongs in the video itself, not only in the caption, and the FTC says a platform's built-in label alone is not enough. As the brand, you are expected to tell creators what to do and check what gets posted.</p>
<p><strong>Rights.</strong> A post on their account is one use. Reposting it on your channels, running it as a paid ad from their handle, or cutting it into other ads are separate uses. Spell out each one, with a term, in a short written agreement. <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-are-usage-rights">What Are Usage Rights?</a> covers the terms, and the <a href="/cinebody-blog/photo-video-usage-rights">full usage rights checklist</a> covers paid usage in detail.</p>

<h2>Step 5: Measure what matters</h2>
<p>Pick the goal before the post goes live, then track against it.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Awareness:</strong> views, reach, saves, and shares, from screenshots of their analytics.</li>
<li><strong>Sales:</strong> clicks and orders through a unique link or discount code for each creator.</li>
<li><strong>Content:</strong> how their video performs when you run it as an ad against your usual creative.</li>
</ul>
<p>Keep notes on who delivered on time, who was easy to work with, and whose audience responded. The best partnerships get better the second and third time, so rebook the good ones before you go looking for new names.</p>
