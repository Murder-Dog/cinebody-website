<p>Remote video production means the people on camera, and usually the people holding the camera, are somewhere you are not. You direct from a desk. Done well, it gets you real footage from many places in the time it takes to book one crew.</p>
<p>The work does not disappear. It moves: more planning up front, clearer direction, faster review. Here is the whole process, start to finish.</p>
<h2>1. Plan the video</h2>
<p>Start with the finished piece, not the shoot. Answer four questions first:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>What are you making?</strong> A 30-second social cut, a recruiting video, an event recap, a set of testimonials.</li>
<li><strong>Where will it run?</strong> This sets orientation and length for every shot. Short-form feeds want vertical. A website player or a presentation usually wants horizontal. <a href="/cinebody-blog/how-to-frame-video-on-smartphones">How to Frame Video on Your Phone</a> covers the details.</li>
<li><strong>Who is on camera?</strong> Employees, customers, fans, athletes.</li>
<li><strong>When do you need it?</strong> Set the delivery date, then work backward to a filming deadline. Open-ended shoots drift.</li>
</ul>
<p>Remote works best for everyday video: many voices, many locations, fast turnaround. It is not a replacement for a large hero production with sets and hired talent.</p>
<h2>2. Write the brief and the shot list</h2>
<p>Write a short brief: what you are making, who is filming, where, and what the edit needs. Then turn it into a shot list of one to five asks, one ask per shot, in plain language, with the must-have footage first.</p>
<p>Two posts cover this step: <a href="/cinebody-blog/start-your-shot-list-from-the-brief">Start Your Shot List From a Brief, Not a Blank Page</a> and <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-is-a-shot-list-and-how-do-i-write-one-for-a-ugc-campaign">What Is a Shot List and How Do I Write One for a UGC Campaign?</a> For cutaways, keep <a href="/cinebody-blog/five-tips-for-great-b-roll-filming">Five B-Roll Shots to Grab Every Time</a> handy.</p>
<h2>3. Recruit your filmers</h2>
<p>The best remote footage comes from people who already have a reason to care: your employees, customers, and fans. They know the product and the place, and they come across as themselves.</p>
<ul>
<li>Recruit more people than you need. Some will not film.</li>
<li>Make the ask feel like an invitation to share something, not homework.</li>
<li>Say exactly what it takes: how many shots, roughly how long, and the deadline.</li>
<li>If the ask is real work, pay for it. Product, a gift card, or a flat fee all work.</li>
<li>Get permission in writing to use what they film. Our post on <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-are-usage-rights">usage rights</a> covers the basics.</li>
</ul>
<h2>4. Coach the capture</h2>
<p>Most filmers are not filmmakers, so direction has to reach them where they are: on a phone, right before they press record.</p>
<p>Send a short checklist with the shot list. Light on the face, not behind it. Grid on, eyes near the top third. Phone steady and at eye level. A quiet room for anything with talking, and the <a href="/cinebody-blog/5-steps-to-recording-interviews-iphone">ABCDE interview checklist</a> for interviews. Full battery and free storage.</p>
<p>Be reachable during the shoot. A quick message that says "turn so the window is beside you" can save a whole reshoot.</p>
<p>This is where Cinebody does the most. Filmers open a link and shoot with no app download. The AI Director checks framing, light, focus, steadiness, and audio live while they film, and you can send a direction mid-shoot that lands on their phone while the camera is up.</p>
<h2>5. Collect and review as footage lands</h2>
<p>Collect the original files. Do not ask people to email footage: personal Gmail accounts cap attachments at 25 MB, and phone video passes that quickly. Use an upload link or a shared drive so you get the full-quality file.</p>
<p>Review the first clips the same day they arrive. Check sound, light, framing, and whether the clip actually answers the ask. If something is off, tell the filmer while they are still on site. Feedback after the shoot means a reshoot.</p>
<p>Mark every clip as usable, maybe, or no. Your editor will thank you. On Cinebody, each clip lands transcribed and scored on audio, stability, focus, exposure, and framing, with a plain verdict, so weak takes get flagged while there is still time to fix them.</p>
<h2>6. Edit</h2>
<figure><img src="/blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-video-production/img-1.webp" alt="A video editing timeline open on a desktop monitor"></figure>
<p>If footage arrives during the shoot, the edit can start during the shoot. Rough out the story from the first strong clips, then fill in as the rest lands.</p>
<ul>
<li>Read or search the transcripts to find the story in what people actually said.</li>
<li>Build around your must-have shot, then cover the cuts with B-roll.</li>
<li>Add captions and music.</li>
<li>Cut a version for each place it will run, in the right orientation and length.</li>
</ul>
<p>Cinebody can build that first cut: best takes in order, B-roll over the seams, captions, and music. Type a note like "get to the hook faster" and it revises just that. Higher tiers export to Premiere, Final Cut, or EDL when your editor wants to finish it by hand.</p>
<h2>7. Deliver</h2>
<p>Deliver every version you promised, named so anyone can find it later. Send it to the approver with one clear question: approve, or what should change? Collect notes from one decision maker so the edit does not ping-pong.</p>
<p>After launch, keep the raw footage organized, along with a record of who filmed and what they agreed to. A good remote shoot usually holds more than one video.</p>
