<p>A community video shoot has a pleasant problem: a lot of footage. Twenty people filming five shots each is a hundred clips, and the one line you need for the cut is in there somewhere. Watching all of it by hand is where most of a production week goes.</p>
<p>Cinebody scores every clip as it lands, so the strongest moments come to the top and the weak takes get flagged while the shoot is still live. Here is what gets scored, what the verdict means, and what happens with it.</p>
<h2>What gets scored</h2>
<p>When a clip arrives, Cinebody transcribes what was said and reviews the clip against the shot it was filmed for. It knows the shot's directions, the orientation it should be in, and your brand guidelines. A good clip is not just a sharp clip. It is a clip that does the job the shot asked for.</p>
<p>Each review includes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A technical breakdown:</strong> focus, exposure, stability, audio, and framing.</li>
<li><strong>An overall score and a verdict:</strong> Great, Good, or Redo.</li>
<li><strong>Plain feedback:</strong> a short summary and what would make the take better.</li>
<li><strong>The best stretch:</strong> where the strongest part of the clip starts and ends.</li>
<li><strong>What is on screen:</strong> a plain description of what the clip shows, like &quot;hands tightening the laces on a trail shoe,&quot; not a label like &quot;lifestyle footage.&quot;</li>
</ul>
<h2>Talking clips and B-roll are judged differently</h2>
<p>A testimonial and a shot of the store at dawn have different jobs, so they are not graded the same way.</p>
<p>For clips where someone talks, delivery counts as much as the picture. A take is only Great if the line is clear, lands as a complete thought, and is worth quoting. A clip that looks beautiful but rambles, restarts, or gets cut off mid-sentence is Good at best, and the feedback says why. The best stretch lands on the strongest complete line, not the prettiest frame.</p>
<p>For B-roll, scenery, and action, the clip is judged on the picture and whether it fits the shot. Silence or room sound is fine and does not pull the score down. Only real audio problems, like wind or handling noise, count. Handheld movement is only marked down when it actually distracts, because phone footage naturally moves a little. (For what makes B-roll useful in the first place, see <a href="/cinebody-blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-capturing-b-roll">Film B-Roll Editors Can Actually Use</a>.)</p>
<h2>What the verdict means</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Great:</strong> use it. Strong picture, and for a talking clip, a complete line worth keeping.</li>
<li><strong>Good:</strong> usable, with something a better take would fix. Often exactly what you need.</li>
<li><strong>Redo:</strong> something is getting in the way, like a blurry subject, a blown-out window, or a line that falls apart. The feedback names it.</li>
</ul>
<p>The verdict is the same everywhere you see the clip: the live dashboard, the Library, the project page, and the editor. One grading language, so nobody has to translate a number.</p>
<p>One more thing matters here. If Cinebody cannot actually review a clip, it leaves the clip unrated instead of filling in a middle-of-the-road score. An empty score is honest. A made-up one would quietly mislead everything downstream.</p>
<h2>How it saves review time</h2>
<p>Scoring changes where you spend your attention.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Start from the top.</strong> Filter to Great clips first when you are hunting for the hero moment.</li>
<li><strong>Search by what people said.</strong> Every clip is transcribed, so you can find &quot;my commute&quot; without scrubbing.</li>
<li><strong>Let the obvious calls happen on their own.</strong> You set a sensitivity for the project. Clear keepers can be approved and clear misses set aside automatically. Everything in between waits for a person, and you can overrule any decision.</li>
<li><strong>Fix it while the shoot is live.</strong> If a filmer's clips keep coming back Redo for the same reason, message them now instead of discovering it next week.</li>
</ul>
<p>You still watch footage. You just watch the right footage first.</p>
<h2>How scores feed the edit</h2>
<p>This is where scoring pays off twice. The review is not a report that sits in a folder. It is what Cinebody's editor reads.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Trims start from the best stretch.</strong> The editor uses the strongest part of each clip instead of cutting blind.</li>
<li><strong>Stronger takes win ties.</strong> When several people filmed the same shot, the higher-scoring takes are preferred.</li>
<li><strong>B-roll lands where it fits.</strong> The plain description of what is on screen helps the editor place B-roll that matches what is being said.</li>
<li><strong>The story comes from the words.</strong> Transcripts let the editor find the real story in what people said, then build a first cut you review.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you pay per clip, scores can also drive rewards. A project can add a bonus for high-scoring clips on top of the per-clip rate, so the people who film the best footage earn more.</p>
<h2>What a score is not</h2>
<p>A score is a strong first opinion, not the final word. Sometimes a Good clip with a slightly shaky frame has the one honest line that makes the whole video. Sometimes the Great clip is technically perfect and says nothing new.</p>
<p>That is why the verdict comes with reasons, and why a person can overrule it. Real people film the footage. Cinebody coaches, scores, and cuts it, so your team spends its time on the calls that need taste.</p>
