<p>Most employees who freeze on camera have plenty to say. The lens turns a normal conversation into a performance, with their name and face attached. The fix is less about acting tips and more about taking the pressure out of the ask.</p>
<p>Your people know the work, the customers, and the stories better than anyone you could hire. Here is how to make it easy for them to share that on video.</p>
<h2>Why people freeze</h2>
<p>Before you fix it, know what you are fixing. The usual causes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>They think it has to be perfect.</strong> They picture a polished ad and know they are not actors.</li>
<li><strong>They are handed words that are not theirs.</strong> Reading lines sounds stiff, and viewers can hear it.</li>
<li><strong>They worry about how it lands at work.</strong> What will their manager, their coworkers, or a future employer think?</li>
<li><strong>They do not know what happens next.</strong> Where it runs, who sees it, and whether they get a say.</li>
<li><strong>There is an audience.</strong> Filming in front of a crowd of coworkers makes anyone self-conscious.</li>
</ul>
<p>Every step below takes aim at one of those.</p>
<h2>Make it optional, and mean it</h2>
<p>People relax when saying no is safe. Invite people. Do not assign them.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Say it out loud.</strong> Taking part is voluntary, and declining will not affect reviews, assignments, or how anyone is treated.</li>
<li><strong>Watch who asks.</strong> An invitation from a direct manager can feel like an order. A note from the project lead to the whole team feels like an offer.</li>
<li><strong>Offer off-camera roles.</strong> Some people would rather film B-roll, film a coworker, or show their hands doing the work while they talk.</li>
<li><strong>Start small.</strong> An internal update or a short social clip is a gentler first step than a video that will run as a paid ad.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Use prompts, not scripts</h2>
<p>A script asks someone to perform. A prompt asks them to remember. Give people questions they can answer from their own day:</p>
<ul>
<li>"Walk me through the first hour of your shift."</li>
<li>"Tell me about a customer you still think about."</li>
<li>"What do people get wrong about your job?"</li>
<li>"Show me the tool you could not do your job without."</li>
</ul>
<p>Share the prompts a day or two ahead so nobody is caught off guard, and tell people not to memorize answers. A few notes in their head are enough. If something must be said a certain way, like a product name or a safety line, keep it to one short sentence and let them say the rest in their own words.</p>
<h2>Build in practice takes</h2>
<p>The first take is rarely the keeper. Saying so up front takes the weight off it.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Call the first one a warm-up.</strong> "This one is just to check the sound" lets people loosen up without it counting.</li>
<li><strong>Ask the same question twice.</strong> The second answer is often shorter and more relaxed, because they have already found the words.</li>
<li><strong>Let them watch it back.</strong> Most people settle once they see they look and sound fine. Let them decide whether to do another.</li>
<li><strong>Keep it short.</strong> A few quick takes beat one long, tense session.</li>
<li><strong>Treat stumbles as normal.</strong> A flub is just a place to cut. Laugh, pause, and pick the sentence back up.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Keep the setup simple</h2>
<p>Gear raises the stakes. The less it looks like a production, the more it feels like a conversation.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Use a phone.</strong> Prop it or put it on a small tripod at eye level.</li>
<li><strong>Film where they work.</strong> Familiar surroundings are calming, and they show the job.</li>
<li><strong>Find a quiet corner with soft light.</strong> Face a window rather than sitting with one behind them.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the room small.</strong> One person asking questions, not a row of onlookers.</li>
<li><strong>Talk to a person, not the lens.</strong> Answering someone just beside the camera feels natural. Save direct-to-camera for formats that need it.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Respect privacy and opt-outs</h2>
<p>People share more when they trust how the footage will be handled.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Explain where it will run before anyone films.</strong> The intranet, the careers page, social, and paid ads are very different audiences.</li>
<li><strong>Get written consent, even from employees.</strong> A plain-language release that covers where and how long the video can run avoids surprises later. <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-are-usage-rights">What Are Usage Rights?</a> explains the terms. This is general guidance, not legal advice.</li>
<li><strong>Check the frame for private details.</strong> Screens, badges, whiteboards, customer information, and coworkers who did not agree to be on camera.</li>
<li><strong>Show people the cut first.</strong> Nobody should see themselves in a company video for the first time on social.</li>
<li><strong>Make it easy to change their mind.</strong> Decide ahead of time what happens if someone asks to be removed, including after they leave the company, and tell them before they film.</li>
<li><strong>Make the connection clear in ads.</strong> The FTC's Endorsement Guides say an ad featuring an employee is misleading unless that connection is made clear. A name and role on screen is a simple way to do it.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Recognize the people who take part</h2>
<p>How you treat the first group decides whether anyone volunteers next time.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Thank them by name.</strong> Publicly if they want that, privately if they do not.</li>
<li><strong>Send them the finished video before it goes out,</strong> so they can share it first if they like.</li>
<li><strong>Tell their manager</strong> what they contributed.</li>
<li><strong>Share what happened.</strong> Where the video ran and what people said about it.</li>
<li><strong>Reward the time, not the message.</strong> If there is a perk for taking part, it should never depend on saying something positive.</li>
<li><strong>Invite them back as guides.</strong> A coworker who has done it once is the best proof that it is not as scary as it looks.</li>
</ul>
