<p>Plenty of brand video plans are a list of big projects and a hope that the rest will happen. Then the launch film eats the budget, the calendar fills with requests nobody planned for, and the feed goes quiet for months at a time.</p>
<p>A good annual plan fixes that before January starts. It sets a few hero pieces, builds a steady everyday stream around them, and decides who makes what, with what money, on what schedule. Here is how to build one in an afternoon or two.</p>

<h2>Audit the last twelve months first</h2>
<p>Before you plan anything new, look at what you already made. Pull every video your team published into one sheet with a few columns:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>What it was.</strong> Launch film, testimonial, event recap, how-to, recruiting clip.</li>
<li><strong>Where it ran.</strong> Paid social, organic feeds, website, email, sales decks, internal.</li>
<li><strong>What it cost.</strong> Money out the door and roughly how many team hours.</li>
<li><strong>How it performed against its job.</strong> Watch time for a feed clip, clicks for an ad, how often sales actually used it.</li>
<li><strong>Would you make it again?</strong> Yes, no, or yes but cheaper.</li>
</ul>
<p>Patterns show up fast. Usually a few formats did the heavy lifting, one expensive piece underdelivered, and a handful of quick clips got reused far more than anyone expected. Also list what people asked for and never got. Those gaps are next year's demand.</p>

<h2>Anchor the year to real dates</h2>
<p>Open a calendar and mark the dates that will happen whether or not you make video: product launches, trade shows and conferences, your own events, seasonal peaks, hiring pushes, annual reports, company anniversaries, and the holidays that matter to your customers.</p>
<p>Then work backward from each one. A hero piece for a spring launch needs its brief written in winter. An event recap needs filmers assigned before the doors open. Put the start date on the calendar, not only the publish date. Videos that miss their moment usually were not badly made. They were started too late.</p>

<h2>Pick a few hero pieces, then plan the stream around them</h2>
<p>Hero pieces are the big swings: the brand film, the launch spot, the campaign that carries paid media. Keep the list short enough that each one gets real attention. Choose them by asking which dates carry real business weight and which messages need a polished, controlled treatment.</p>
<p>The everyday stream is everything between them: employee stories, customer clips, behind the scenes, quick answers, recaps. It keeps you visible and supplies the proof that hero pieces cannot. Give each hero piece a stream that builds toward it and one that follows it, so the launch is not a single post and then silence.</p>
<p>We cover how to schedule that everyday lane week to week, with cadence and metrics, in <a href="/cinebody-blog/why-lo-fi-content-should-be-part-of-your-video-content-plan">How to Fit Lo-Fi Video Into Your Content Plan</a>. The annual plan only needs to reserve the room for it.</p>

<h2>Choose recurring formats and name an owner for each</h2>
<p>Formats turn a year of video into something a team can actually run. Pick three to five that fill the gaps from your audit. For example: a monthly customer testimonial, a quarterly employee spotlight, a recap for every event, and a short answer to a real customer question every two weeks.</p>
<p>For each format, write one line: what it is, how often, where it runs, and who owns it. An owner is a person, not a department. If nobody's name is on it, it will not happen in March.</p>
<p>Keep formats simple enough to brief quickly. A short brief and a list of one to five filmable asks is enough. See <a href="/cinebody-blog/start-your-shot-list-from-the-brief">Start Your Shot List From a Brief, Not a Blank Page</a>.</p>

<h2>Decide who films what</h2>
<p>Match the maker to the job, and write it into the plan so nobody scrambles later.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A production partner or crew</strong> for hero pieces that need lighting, sets, hired talent, or precise product work.</li>
<li><strong>Employees</strong> for spotlights, behind the scenes, and how-tos. They are close to the work and easy to reach.</li>
<li><strong>Customers and fans</strong> for testimonials and the product in real life.</li>
<li><strong>Your marketing team or on-site staff</strong> for events and anything fast.</li>
</ul>
<p>For the everyday stream, plan how you will coach people who are not filmmakers and how you will collect permissions. <a href="/cinebody-blog/creating-video-content-without-a-production-crew">How to Make Brand Video Without a Production Crew</a> covers the first, and <a href="/cinebody-blog/what-are-usage-rights">What Are Usage Rights?</a> covers the second. If your filmers are spread across cities, <a href="/cinebody-blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-video-production">The Ultimate Guide to Remote Video Production</a> walks through the whole process.</p>

<h2>Budget time and money, not just money</h2>
<p>Split the budget into the two lanes. Hero pieces get project budgets with a line for each part: creative, production, talent, music, edit, and the paid media behind it. The everyday stream gets a monthly amount for incentives, small gear, tools, and editing.</p>
<p>Then budget hours. For each format, estimate the time to brief, film, review, edit, and approve one episode. Multiply by how often it runs, and check the total against the hours your team really has. If the math does not fit, cut a format now instead of letting three of them quietly fail by summer.</p>
<p>Hold back a small reserve of money and team time for what you cannot plan: a customer story that lands out of nowhere, a news moment, a request from leadership.</p>

<h2>Set a review rhythm</h2>
<p>A plan written in December and never opened again is a wish list. Put the check-ins on the calendar now.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Monthly:</strong> fifteen minutes per format. Did it ship, how did it do, what is next.</li>
<li><strong>Quarterly:</strong> retire formats that are not working, add one from the backlog, and move budget toward what performs.</li>
<li><strong>After each hero piece:</strong> what it cost, what it drove, and which everyday clips around it pulled their weight.</li>
</ul>
<p>Keep the audit sheet going all year. Next December, the audit is already done.</p>
