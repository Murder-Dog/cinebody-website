<p>The best testimonial is a customer telling the truth in their own words. A crew, a light kit, and strangers in the room are often what stop that from happening. A phone in the customer's own kitchen, shop, or office usually gets you closer.</p>
<p>Here is how to run a testimonial where the customer does the filming and your team handles the plan, the coaching, and the edit.</p>
<h2>Find customers who will actually talk</h2>
<p>You are not looking for your biggest account. You are looking for someone with a specific story who is willing to tell it.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ask the people who talk to customers.</strong> Sales, support, and account managers know who sends thank-you notes and who has a before-and-after story.</li>
<li><strong>Read what people already said.</strong> Reviews, survey comments, and support threads that ended well point to customers who already have words for their experience.</li>
<li><strong>Look for specifics.</strong> "It got our Friday inventory count done before lunch" makes a better video than "It's great."</li>
<li><strong>Mix it up.</strong> Different roles, business sizes, and places make a set of testimonials more believable than five people who sound alike.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Make the ask easy to say yes to</h2>
<p>Keep the first ask short and personal, ideally from the person who already has the relationship. Tell them:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>What you are making</strong> and where it will run.</li>
<li><strong>How long it takes.</strong> "About 20 minutes on your phone" is an easy yes. "A video shoot" sounds like a lost afternoon.</li>
<li><strong>That they film where they are,</strong> when it suits them.</li>
<li><strong>That there is no script,</strong> and they can skip any question they are not comfortable with.</li>
<li><strong>What they get for it, if anything,</strong> and that it does not depend on what they say. Thank people for their time, never for a positive opinion.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Ask questions that get real answers</h2>
<p>Scripts make customers sound like ads. Open questions get stories. Send them one at a time and let the customer talk.</p>
<ol>
<li>What was going on before you found us?</li>
<li>What made you decide to try it?</li>
<li>When did you first notice it was working?</li>
<li>What is different now, day to day?</li>
<li>Was there anything that surprised you, good or bad?</li>
<li>What would you tell someone in your position who is on the fence?</li>
</ol>
<p>A few habits make the answers usable. Ask them to repeat the question in their answer ("Before we found them, we were...") so each clip stands on its own in the edit. When an answer stays general, follow up with "Can you give me an example?" And leave room for the imperfect parts. A customer who mentions a learning curve and then explains why it was worth it is more believable than one with nothing but praise.</p>
<h2>Help them film well at home or at work</h2>
<p>Customers are not filmmakers, and they do not need to be. Send a short guide before they start:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sound first.</strong> A quiet room beats a pretty one. Turn off fans and TVs, close the windows, and keep the phone close. A plug-in or wireless mic helps if they have one.</li>
<li><strong>Face the light.</strong> Sit facing a window, not with one behind them.</li>
<li><strong>Steady and at eye level.</strong> A stack of books or a small tripod beats holding the phone.</li>
<li><strong>Their space, tidied.</strong> Their shop floor, desk, or kitchen tells part of the story. Just clear the clutter and anything private from the frame.</li>
<li><strong>Grab some B-roll.</strong> Ten seconds of them using the product or doing the work gives the editor something to cut to.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Get the release before you publish</h2>
<p>Filming a clip for you is not the same as permission to run it in an ad. Get a signed release that says, in plain words, where the video can run (your site, social, paid ads), for how long, whether you can edit it, and whether their name, title, and company can appear. If other people, a workplace, or someone else's logo show up in the frame, those may need clearing too. Minors need a parent or guardian to sign.</p>
<p><a href="/cinebody-blog/what-are-usage-rights">What Are Usage Rights?</a> explains the key terms, and the <a href="/cinebody-blog/photo-video-usage-rights">photo and video usage rights checklist</a> covers releases, locations, music, and logos in detail. This is general guidance, not legal advice.</p>
<h2>Edit a testimonial that stays believable</h2>
<p>The FTC's Endorsement Guides say endorsements "must reflect the honest opinions, findings, beliefs, or experience of the endorser," and that an endorsement may not be presented out of context or reworded in a way that distorts the person's opinion or experience. You can tighten a testimonial. You cannot change what it says.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cut for length, not meaning.</strong> Removing filler words and a tangent is fine. Joining half of one sentence to half of another to build a claim they never made is not.</li>
<li><strong>Keep quotes exact.</strong> If you put their words on screen in quotation marks, use what they actually said.</li>
<li><strong>Keep some texture.</strong> A pause, a laugh, a real room. Polish past that point and a customer starts to look like an actor.</li>
<li><strong>Watch results claims.</strong> If a customer describes results most people will not get, the FTC expects the ad to make clear what people can generally expect.</li>
<li><strong>Disclose connections viewers would not expect.</strong> The FTC says a connection such as payment or something of value given to the endorser should usually be disclosed clearly, and an ad featuring a relative or employee is misleading unless that connection is made clear.</li>
<li><strong>Show it to them before it runs.</strong> It catches mistakes and keeps the relationship good.</li>
</ul>
